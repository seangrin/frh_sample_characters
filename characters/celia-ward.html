Powers

  • ESP and aura sensitivity: Celia can read haunted pressure, residue, emotional wrongness, and the supernatural trace of bad rooms.
  • Mediumistic gifts: her real strength is human-scale contact with grief, spirits, and the recently dead rather than grand ceremonial authority.

How Celia Uses Her Powers

  • She reads grief, residue, hauntings, lies, and spiritual contamination where other investigators only see noise, melodrama, or poverty.
  • She is strongest in mourning rooms, tenements, church basements, parlors, seance spaces, fraud cases, and recent supernatural trauma.
  • She turns supernatural confusion into actionable human truth, especially when the problem is emotional, repetitive, fragmentary, and ugly.
  • She is a field-side counterpart to more scholarly or cosmological interpreters: less abstract, dirtier, and more immediately usable.

Character Overview

Celia Ward made her life in the places respectable people prefer not to look too closely: mourning rooms, cheap flats where the dead still seemed present, church basements full of casseroles and rumor, back parlors where grief and fraud sat too near each other, and seance circles where somebody was always trying to steal hope from the desperate. She was never meant to be a grand velvet-curtain authority on the dead. Her gift is narrower, messier, and more personal than that. The recently dead cling to her. Hauntings leave residue she can feel in her teeth. Rooms full of grief, shame, lies, and unfinished business talk to her whether she wants them to or not. What she gets is fragmentary, emotional, repetitive, and often infuriatingly human, but it is usually enough to tell her where the real hurt is.

Age gave Celia patience, but not softness. She moves through the world in shawls, saint medals, layered skirts, sensible boots, and enough hidden pockets to worry a customs officer, with incense following her like a cloud of warning. Rich clients, gutter thieves, widows, undertakers, and fake spiritualists all discover the same thing sooner or later: she is harder to move than they expected. She can read a con, read a mourner, and read a haunting, and she has made a private crusade out of humiliating charlatans who make money off the bereaved. She is devastatingly practical about sorrow. She knows who in the neighborhood is lying, who is frightened, which undertaker can be trusted, which room smells wrong before sunset, and which spirit is only noise and which one is trying to tell the truth badly.

Celia is most useful when the party needs somebody to turn supernatural mess into something human and actionable. She is not Edgar's rival and not a cosmological lecturer. She is his field-side counterpart: the haunted witness interface, the grief-and-residue specialist, the woman who can give a sharper, dirtier, more usable answer when a clean symbolic reading is not enough. In play, her humor matters, but the steel under it matters more. She has the force of nature quality of a woman who has spent years around loss without becoming sentimental, who cannot bear grief thieves, and who will walk straight into a bad room because somebody in it needs help and nobody else is going to listen correctly.
