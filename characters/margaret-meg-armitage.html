Margaret "Meg" Armitage

Disciplined inventor and occult technologist who meets mystery with method - the woman who sees the world's hidden systems and cannot stop testing where they lead.

Powers

  • Gadgeteer [50] and High Technology 1 (Effective TL 7) [20]: Meg can design, build, and analyze technology a decade ahead of 1923's baseline. This is not merely exceptional engineering - it is driven by something that exceeds normal inspiration.
  • Weird Science-18 [12]: the operational expression of her impossible-device lane. She can analyze, reverse-engineer, and theorize about occult apparatus, relic housings, broken wards in machinery, and prototype devices that know more than their makers do.
  • Magery 1 [15] with tech-magic spell suite: her spells are entirely machine- and material-oriented - Machine Control, Machine Speech, Seek Machine, Schematic, Glitch, Conduct Power, Stop Power, and related. The magic is an extension of her engineering intuition, not a separate supernatural mode.
  • Unusual Background (dream-tech flashes and impossible engineering intuition) [10]: her gift is described as coming in flashes. She does not always understand how she knows what she knows.

Required Secret Power

Impossible-device and occult-hardware lane (grouped):

  • Gadgeteer [50] - the core capacity that exceeds any natural human engineering ability
  • High Technology 1 [20] - effective TL 7 in a TL 6 world; her equipment exists ahead of the timeline
  • Magery 1 [15] - the occult layer that lets her interface with magical and hybrid apparatus
  • Weird Science-18 [12] - the skill that operationalizes the impossible-device lane
  • Unusual Background (dream-tech flashes and impossible engineering intuition) [10] - the experiential signal that something beyond training is producing her insights

Secret (Builder of impossible devices and occult hardware) [-20]: discovery would expose her to scientific exploitation, institutional suppression, and supernatural targeting simultaneously. A woman in 1923 with a TL 7 workshop is not a curiosity - she is a target.

Required Secret Society

No named non-FRH hidden order appears in the source sheet.

The source lists no Patron advantage beyond FRH, and no Secret disadvantage naming a specific non-FRH organization. Meg's hidden social infrastructure - if any - might exist through her access to black-market workshops, occult hardware networks, or proto-weird-science communities, but nothing is formalized in the sheet.

This may be a deliberate design choice - she operates more as a solo researcher than as a society member. Or it may be a sheet gap. The current page preserves the source as written and flags this for later human review.

FRH does not satisfy the secret-society requirement.

How Meg Uses Her Powers

  • She is the party's apparatus analyst - the person who can determine not just that something is wrong with a device, but how it is wrong, what it was meant to do, and what terrible compromise made it do this instead.
  • Her machine-oriented spell suite (Machine Control-16, Machine Speech-16, Schematic-16, Reveal Function-16) lets her interrogate occult hardware directly, bypassing the need for a human informant or document trail.
  • Weird Science-18 gives her a reliable channel to understand things that precede their own theoretical framework. She does not need to know the cosmology to start reading the apparatus.
  • Her TL 7 access means she can build equipment that is operationally impossible in 1923 - communications gear, armament components, containment systems, or diagnostic instruments that no legitimate supply chain can produce.
  • She is strongest in stories where mystery does not cancel technique: black-market workshops, relic housings, broken wards in machinery, impossible radios, containment failures, and prototype devices that know more than their makers do.

Why She Works

Meg proves that impossible engineering occupies a lane most occult settings mishandle. She does not turn magic into mere mechanics. She proves the two can overlap without becoming the same thing - and she is most useful precisely where no one else in the party knows what to call what they are looking at.

Character Overview

Margaret "Meg" Armitage belongs to that rare and dangerous class of people who look at an impossible machine and feel curiosity before fear. From early on she was the sort of mind that wanted not only to know whether something worked, but how, why, under what hidden principles, and what else those principles might permit if pushed one step further. Ordinary engineering was never quite enough for her, because ordinary engineering assumed the world was only as strange as textbooks admitted. Meg learned otherwise. Somewhere between workshop discipline, experimental nerve, and occult-system pressure, she became a woman who could meet mystery with method instead of surrendering either one.

That is why she feels so valuable in FRH. Meg occupies a lane many occult settings mishandle: impossible engineer, occult technologist, proto-weird-science investigator. She makes laboratories, field rigs, prototypes, hidden systems, sabotage, and dangerous apparatus feel not like a genre intrusion but like another honest way the supernatural becomes legible. She is strongest in stories where mystery does not cancel technique: black-market workshops, relic housings, broken wards embedded in machinery, impossible radios, trap logic, containment failures, improvised occult hardware, and prototype devices that know more than their makers do. She does not turn magic into mere mechanics. She proves the two can overlap without becoming the same thing.

What keeps Meg honest is that her best scenes are rarely about instantly building a perfect answer. They are about inference, prototype failure, dangerous insight, and the thrilling moment of understanding what a thing is doing before anyone else even knows what cosmology it belongs to. She arrives slightly too early for her century and knows it. That gives her a natural friction with respectable rooms, slower minds, and institutions that would rather misfile the anomaly than let her open it up on the bench. In party play she becomes a rigorous clue vector for apparatus and process, the person who can say not just that something is wrong, but how it is wrong, what it was meant to do, and what terrible compromise made it do this instead.

For a new companion, Meg should read as a disciplined inventor with a field operator's nerve, not a steampunk mascot or a cackling ray-gun eccentric. She is the kind of woman who sees the world's hidden systems and cannot stop testing where they lead, even when the answer turns out to be morally worse, mechanically stranger, or more useful than anyone should be comfortable with.
