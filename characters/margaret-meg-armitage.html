Description

Margaret "Meg" Armitage belongs to that rare and dangerous class of people who look at an impossible machine and feel curiosity before fear. From early on she was the sort of mind that wanted not only to know whether something worked, but how, why, under what hidden principles, and what else those principles might permit if pushed one step further. Ordinary engineering was never quite enough for her, because ordinary engineering assumed the world was only as strange as textbooks admitted. Meg learned otherwise. Somewhere between workshop discipline, experimental nerve, and occult-system pressure, she became a woman who could meet mystery with method instead of surrendering either one.

That is why she feels so valuable in FRH. Meg occupies a lane many occult settings mishandle: impossible engineer, occult technologist, proto-weird-science investigator. She makes laboratories, field rigs, prototypes, hidden systems, sabotage, and dangerous apparatus feel not like a genre intrusion but like another honest way the supernatural becomes legible. She is strongest in stories where mystery does not cancel technique: black-market workshops, relic housings, broken wards embedded in machinery, impossible radios, trap logic, containment failures, improvised occult hardware, and prototype devices that know more than their makers do. She does not turn magic into mere mechanics. She proves the two can overlap without becoming the same thing.

What keeps Meg honest is that her best scenes are rarely about instantly building a perfect answer. They are about inference, prototype failure, dangerous insight, and the thrilling moment of understanding what a thing is doing before anyone else even knows what cosmology it belongs to. She arrives slightly too early for her century and knows it. That gives her a natural friction with respectable rooms, slower minds, and institutions that would rather misfile the anomaly than let her open it up on the bench. In party play she becomes a rigorous clue vector for apparatus and process, the person who can say not just that something is wrong, but how it is wrong, what it was meant to do, and what terrible compromise made it do this instead.

For a new companion, Meg should read as a disciplined inventor with a field operator's nerve, not a steampunk mascot or a cackling ray-gun eccentric. She is the kind of woman who sees the world's hidden systems and cannot stop testing where they lead, even when the answer turns out to be morally worse, mechanically stranger, or more useful than anyone should be comfortable with.
