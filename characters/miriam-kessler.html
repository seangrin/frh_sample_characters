Description

Miriam Kessler has the sort of face and manner that towns teach themselves not to remember too closely. She is a county schoolteacher and part-time registrar at Liberty's public library, mild enough in public that people talk too freely around her and precise enough in private that she notices what they were hoping would blur together. That plainness is not the absence of character. It is part of the engine of the woman. Miriam has made a life in records, copybooks, ledgers, property transfers, and the slow administrative surfaces on which a town tells the truth about itself only by accident.

Somewhere along the line those surfaces began to answer back. Miriam does not possess broad clairvoyance or theatrical visions. What she has is narrower and in some ways worse: a records-bound faculty that turns repetition into pressure and pressure into knowledge. When she studies archives, maps, school papers, or town ledgers too long, the hidden pattern begins to bleed through. Disappearances stop looking isolated. Scandals stop looking accidental. Margins and dates begin to feel like nerves. The town starts to confess itself in fragments. That is what makes Miriam dangerous. She is not merely cataloguing records; she is discovering that Liberty may be organized around erasure, and once she knows that, her sense of duty becomes very simple. The missing and the dead must be accounted for properly.

That motive keeps her grounded. Miriam is not an occult adventuress in a library skirt. She is a civic-pattern medium, a woman whose power comes through institutions and whose morality is tied to the names that go missing inside them. Her hidden life is therefore quiet, patient, and full of dread. She builds indexes no one asked for. She notices family names recurring where they should not. She listens when archives feel heavier than paper ought to feel. And she keeps moving through schoolrooms and public desks with the outward forgettability of someone who understands that plainness can be one of the safest places from which to wage a war against a town's deeper machinery.

For a new player, Miriam should feel less like a psychic librarian and more like the personification of bureaucratic haunting. She is the one who studies the ledger until the ledger starts staring back, and once that happens she cannot go on pretending that official records are neutral things. They have become the medium through which the town's conscience, or its guilt, has decided to speak.
