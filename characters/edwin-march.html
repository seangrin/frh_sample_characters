Description

Before the war, Edwin March was the sort of man history is usually happy to use up without remembering. He came out of industrial labor, knew machines by their noise and their temper, and understood the dignity of work in terms of weight, heat, grease, and wages honestly earned. Then the Great War took that practical life and fed it into the infantry. Edwin crossed from factory discipline into trench discipline the way thousands of men did: not as a hero from the beginning, but as a workingman who learned mud, wire, bombardment, fatigue, and the long education of bad orders.

What came home from the war was not exactly the same man who had gone out. Edwin survived too much, too closely, and in the wrong conditions. Somewhere between trench rot, shellfire, ruin, and whatever impossible thing the war opened in him, his body learned a second answer to danger. Under enough pressure it becomes something inhuman: a trench-born organism of survival, hunger, and mutilated instinct. Edwin does not think of that form as another life. It is the same wound wearing another shape, the same man dragged into a state he fears, hides, and sometimes needs. What makes him tragic is not that he turns monstrous, but that the monstrous thing is still built out of the laborer and soldier he once was.

That is why Edwin remains so grounded even at his strangest. He still thinks like a practical man. He notices tools, vehicles, weak points, supply problems, and the quality of a room before he notices anyone's grand theory. He knows what bodies can endure and what they cannot. He has the fellowship of damaged veterans, the wariness of a man who does not entirely trust his own skin, and the habits of someone who has spent years trying to stay useful while concealing a truth that would turn him into a specimen, a scandal, or a hunted thing. The Royal British Legion gives him one hidden kind of belonging; the Followers of the Right Hand give him another. Neither can give him back the body or the war he lost.

To a new companion, Edwin should read first as a British infantry survivor with a worker's hands and a survivor's stillness, and only then as a man with a terrible second form. He is not a monster with a human disguise. He is a human being who should have come home damaged and instead came home altered, carrying the sort of war that does not stay in memory but moves into flesh.
