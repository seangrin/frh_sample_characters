Description

Gabriel Voss was not born into the world in the ordinary sense, and that fact has defined every year of his long existence. He was made in Eastern Europe more than a century ago by hands that treated flesh, chemistry, will, and trespass as parts of the same experiment. Whatever name he had at the beginning, whatever body first supplied the material, whatever conscience the maker claimed in justification, all of that is buried now under the harder truth of continuity. Gabriel endures. He does not breathe, does not eat, does not sleep, and does not heal as other men do. When he is torn, he is not comforted by nature. He is repaired. His very body is an argument someone once made against the limits of life.

That could easily have made him grotesque in the shallow sense, but Gabriel is strongest in the opposite direction. He is grave, patient, durable, and deeply, stubbornly human in the places that matter. He has lived too long as an object of making, maintenance, speculation, or fear to treat personhood lightly. He knows what it means to be handled, classified, and misunderstood. He knows the difference between a wound and a failing seam, between exhaustion and structural damage, between being pitied and being recognized. In FRH he intensifies a core question the setting already wants to ask: what counts as a person in a world where soul, craft, belief, violence, and trespass can all leave fingerprints on the body?

Gabriel's life naturally draws the world around him into makers' journals, clandestine laboratories, private patrons, occult manufacture, and the ethics of repair. Yet he is not merely a walking problem statement. In party play he tends to become a moral and social continuity engine, the person who keeps a case from becoming only a puzzle or only a hunt. He understands damage in the practical sense and violation in the metaphysical sense, and he is difficult to impress with bad theories or fashionable panic. His power is quiet protector energy, not theatrical monstrosity. The more frightened a room becomes, the steadier he tends to look.

A new companion should understand Gabriel first as a man who has outlived the right of the experiment to continue and continues anyway. He is not an action figure stitched together out of horror props. He is a patient created being carrying old violence in a maintained body, trying to decide what decency looks like after manufacture, survival, and history have all had their turn at him.
