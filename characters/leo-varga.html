Description

Leo Varga learned the occult from behind the stage. Before anything impossible happened to him, he was already a backstage electrician and apparatus man for prestige illusionists, spirit-show frauds, and electrical marvel acts that promised the crowd a glimpse of the future while quietly hoping not to electrocute the chorus girl. That labor mattered. Leo understood wires, rigging, circuits, catwalks, trap logic, exits, and the difference between a miracle and a switch hidden under velvet. He came out of working rooms, not drawing rooms, and he kept the instincts of a laboring man even after the supernatural finally put its hands on him.

What changed him was not a clean revelation but a catastrophe. A Tesla-style demonstration went wrong in the public, theatrical, ugly way such things do when spectacle outruns caution. Impossible current grounded itself through Leo's body and left him alive for reasons that no one involved could explain honestly. From that point on he became a living discharge path, a man whose body never again behaved like a merely human electrical system. He smells rooms for ozone before he trusts them. He checks exits and breaker panels automatically. He despises fraudulent seances, distrusts showmen, and understands better than most people that the border between machinery and miracle is usually crossed by someone trying to sell tickets.

That is why Leo works so well in FRH. His powers do not float free from his class position or his habits. He is still a backstage worker first: watch the wires, know the exits, keep one hand on the breaker when miracles start talking. The hidden brotherhood of electric showmen that shadows his life only deepens that truth. He belongs to a world of dangerous performers, apparatus builders, riggers, and men who understand that public wonder often has a service corridor full of burned hands behind it. When the supernatural becomes real in front of him, his first instinct is not reverence. It is diagnosis.

For a new player, Leo should feel like a living dynamo who never stopped being a laborer. He is useful because he can read fraud, wiring, failure, and electrical wrongness with the same hard practicality. The impossible did not ennoble him. It just forced a stage electrician to survive at a voltage no working man should ever have been asked to carry.
